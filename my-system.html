2. The spec we chose and why it matters

Spec

0.6 ≤ ζ ≤ 0.8 is the practical range from the lecture. ωn then follows from the settling time: ωn = 4/(ζ·ts).

Why this spec matters for our project

  • Zero steady-state error (I action). The two wheels must turn at the same speed, or the mouse curves. Any speed error between them adds up to a heading error over the 18.2 cm cells. On the slick plywood floor we cannot rely on the walls to correct it. Our motors also run from a 9 V battery whose voltage drops under load, which changes K during a run. Only the integral term removes the error that this drift causes.
  • 0.6 ≤ ζ ≤ 0.8 (we chose ζ = 0.7). The overshoot stays small. Overshooting the target speed on one wheel turns the mouse towards a wall in a corridor only 16.8 cm wide, and a hard speed spike makes the wheels slip on the slick floor (the project brief warns about both).
  • Settling time well below the time to cross one cell. M5 asks us to stop at the centre of the fifth cell within ±10 mm. If the wheel speed has not settled while the mouse crosses a cell, the encoder distance and the position estimate drift.
  • PWM within 0–100 %, with margin. Above 100 % the driver saturates and the linear design no longer holds. Because the 9 V battery sags, we keep the steady-state PWM well below 100 % (about 70 % at most), so the controller still has room to correct as the battery runs down.

3. Final gains, simulated response and Arduino code

For G(s) = K/(τs + 1) with a PI controller the closed loop is τs² + (1 + K·Kp)s + K·Ki = 0. Matching it to s² + 2ζωns + ωn² gives Kp = (2ζωnτ − 1)/K and Ki = ωn²τ/K.
